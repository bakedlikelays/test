# How to Choose (and Hire) the Right Web Designer for Your Local Business

Most advice about hiring a web designer comes down to "ask good questions." That's fine as far as it goes. I've written a whole post of [questions to ask before you sign](/insights/questions-to-ask-a-web-designer-before-you-sign). The trouble is that every designer has a good answer ready. "Yes, it'll be mobile-friendly." "Of course you'll own it." "SEO is built in."

This post is about what comes after the answer: **how to check it.** A web designer's work is public. Their own website is public. Their past clients' websites are public. You can learn more in half an hour with a phone and a free speed test than in an hour on a sales call.

## First, the Hidden Costs of the DIY Route

If you're reading this, you're probably past the DIY stage or close to it. (If you're still deciding, my [honest take on Wix, Squarespace or hiring someone](/insights/diy-website-builders-vs-hiring-northern-arizona) covers when DIY is the right call.) The costs of DIY that owners tell me about afterwards are rarely the subscription fee. They're these:

- **The time sink nobody budgets for.** The first draft takes a weekend. The next six months take an evening here and an evening there: swapping photos, fixing a menu that broke on phones, working out why the contact form stopped working. Count those hours at your billable rate and the "free" site is often the most expensive one you've had.
- **Template walls.** Every template is built for a generic business. The day you need something specific, like a service-area page for each town, a quote form that asks the right questions or a gallery that loads fast on a rural connection, you find out what the template won't let you do.
- **"Mobile-friendly" isn't the same as mobile-optimized.** Builders shrink a desktop layout to fit a phone. They don't rethink it for a thumb. That's why a phone number ends up four scrolls down and the "Book Now" button sits behind a cookie banner.
- **Add-on creep.** Forms, booking, SEO tools, extra storage and email each add a few dollars a month. A year later you're paying agency-level money for a site you still maintain yourself.

None of this means DIY is wrong. It means "cheap" and "low cost" aren't the same thing.

## What to Look For: Beyond "Making It Pretty"

Every designer's portfolio looks good. That's the point of a portfolio. What separates a designer who brings in business from one who makes nice pictures comes down to two skills you can test.

**1. Do they understand conversion?** Conversion rate optimization (CRO) is the practice of getting more visitors to take the action you want: call, book, request a quote. A designer who understands it will talk about *your customer's next step* before they talk about colors and fonts.

**2. Do they understand local search?** For a local business, most new customers start on Google, often on a phone, often with a town name in the search. A designer who understands local search will think about page structure, service pages, location signals and your Google Business Profile from the first conversation, not as a paid extra after launch.

Here's how to check both without taking anyone's word for it.

### The 30-Minute Portfolio Test

Pick three sites from the designer's portfolio, ideally local businesses like yours, and do this for each one:

1. **Open it on your phone, not your laptop.** Can you find the phone number and tap to call within five seconds? Is the main button visible without scrolling? Is the text readable without zooming?
2. **Run it through [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/).** It's free and it's Google's own tool. Look at the *mobile* score. You don't need a perfect 100, but a portfolio full of red scores tells you speed isn't a priority for this designer.
3. **Search for the business the way a customer would.** Try "[service] in [town]," not the company name. Does the site show up? Portfolio sites that are invisible on Google are a warning sign if you're hiring for local visibility.
4. **Check whether the site is still live, and still theirs.** Sites that have disappeared, been rebuilt by someone else or sit on an expired domain tell you something about how the relationship ended.
5. **Call the business.** I don't mean a reference the designer handed you. Call the business itself and ask the owner whether the website brings in work. (My post on [vetting references](/insights/how-to-vet-web-designer-references) goes deeper on what to ask.)

Then run the same test on **the designer's own website.** If it's slow, hard to use on a phone, or you can't tell what they do or where they work, you're seeing the standard they hold themselves to.

## The 5 Critical Questions, and How to Verify Each Answer

You've probably seen these questions before. What matters is what a credible answer looks like and how you can check it.

### 1. "Will my site be fully mobile-responsive and optimized for speed?"

**A weak answer:** "Yes, all our sites are responsive."

**A credible answer** includes a target and a method, such as "we aim for a mobile PageSpeed score in the green and we test on real phones before launch," plus what they do about the usual causes of slowness: image sizes, heavy page builders and third-party scripts.

**How to verify:** You already have the evidence from the portfolio test. Ask them to explain one of the lower scores you found. A good designer will know why it happened and won't get defensive.

### 2. "Who owns the domain, hosting and source code once it's built?"

**A weak answer:** "You do, of course," with nothing in writing.

**A credible answer** says the domain is registered in *your* name with *your* login, says whether the site can be moved to another host, and says what happens to the files if you stop paying for maintenance. Ownership is the topic people most often get burned on. I've covered it in detail in [who really owns your website](/insights/who-really-owns-your-website).

**How to verify:** Ask to see the ownership clause in the contract *before* you sign. Then look up the domain on a WHOIS lookup tool for one of their past clients. If the registrant is the design company rather than the client, that's how they work.

### 3. "How does the design structure support future SEO?"

This question reveals the most, because most designers haven't thought about it.

**A weak answer:** "We install an SEO plugin," or "We'll add your keywords."

**A credible answer** talks about *structure*: one dedicated page for each core service rather than one long "Services" page, a sensible URL hierarchy, room for location pages, clean heading order, internal links between related pages, and schema markup. It should also mention a plan for redirects if you're replacing an old site, because redesigns are one of the most common ways businesses [lose rankings they already had](/insights/website-redesign-without-losing-google-rankings).

**How to verify:** Ask them to sketch a sitemap for *your* business on the call. Even a rough one shows whether they think in structure or only in screens.

### 4. "What is your process for gathering content and handling revisions?"

**A weak answer:** "Just send us your text and photos whenever you're ready."

**A credible answer** names who writes the copy (you, them or a mix), what format they need it in, when they need it, how many revision rounds are included and what counts as a revision versus new work. Content is the most common reason projects stall. A designer with a real process will raise it before you do. (For your side of that, see [what your web designer needs from you](/insights/what-your-web-designer-needs-from-you).)

**How to verify:** Ask for a sample project schedule from a past job. If content deadlines and revision rounds aren't on it, they aren't being managed.

### 5. "Do you offer post-launch support and maintenance?"

**A weak answer:** "Sure, just email us if anything comes up."

**A credible answer** spells out what's covered (updates, backups, security monitoring, small content edits), how fast they respond, what it costs each month and what happens if you cancel. For a fuller picture, read [what great post-launch support looks like](/insights/what-great-post-launch-support-looks-like).

**How to verify:** Ask a past client how long it took to get a small change made six months after launch. That answer tells you more than any service agreement.

## Putting It Together: A Simple Scorecard

When you're comparing two or three designers, score each one from 1 to 3 on:

| Criterion | What you're checking |
|---|---|
| Mobile experience | Portfolio test on your phone |
| Speed | PageSpeed mobile scores across their work |
| Local visibility | Do their clients' sites show up for real searches? |
| Structural SEO thinking | Could they sketch your sitemap? |
| Ownership in writing | Contract clause, WHOIS check |
| Process clarity | Content plan, revision limits, timeline |
| Support after launch | Defined scope, real response times |

The highest total usually isn't the cheapest quote or the flashiest portfolio. It's the designer whose answers held up when you checked them.

## The Bottom Line

A good web designer for a local business is part designer, part salesperson for *your* business and part technician. You can't judge any of those from a pitch, but you can judge them from the work they've already done. Spend 30 minutes testing before you spend a dollar.
