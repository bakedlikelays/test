# The Ultimate Website Discovery Checklist: What to Figure Out Before Building

I've written before about [what a good discovery call with a designer should cover](/insights/what-a-website-discovery-call-should-cover). That post is about judging the person across the table. This one is about **your homework**: the thinking you can do on your own, at the kitchen table, before you talk to anyone.

Whether you plan to build the site yourself or hire someone, the answers below will decide whether your website brings in business or just exists. They'll also save you money, because unclear thinking is the most expensive thing to fix halfway through a build.

## Stop Asking "What Pages Do You Want?"

Most website projects start with a list: Home, About, Services, Gallery, Contact. Then come colors, then a site the owner likes the look of.

That's backwards. Pages and looks are *outputs*. They should fall out of decisions about **what you want visitors to do** and **what they need to see before they'll do it**. Two plumbing companies can need very different websites. One gets most of its work from emergency calls at 11 p.m. The other books scheduled remodels three months out. Same trade, different visitors, different job for the website.

So set aside the page list and work through the questions below. Write the answers down. Short, blunt answers are better than polished ones.

## Core Strategy Questions Every Website Needs to Answer

### 1. What is the *one* primary action you want a visitor to take?

Not three. One. Pick from:

- **Call** (urgent or high-trust services: emergency repair, towing, medical)
- **Book** (appointment businesses: salons, consultations, inspections)
- **Buy** (retail, e-commerce, gift cards, online ordering)
- **Fill out a form** (quote requests, project enquiries, applications)
- **Visit** (restaurants, shops, venues: directions and hours are the action)

You can and should offer secondary actions. But a website that gives equal weight to "Call us, email us, follow us on Instagram, sign up for our newsletter, read our blog" gives the visitor a choice to make, and people who are asked to choose often choose to leave.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] My primary action is: ________
- [ ] My one secondary action (for people not ready yet) is: ________
- [ ] I know what happens *after* that action (who answers the phone, how fast forms get a reply)

That last box matters more than people expect. A site that converts beautifully into a voicemail box nobody checks is a leaky bucket.

### 2. Who exactly is the visitor, and what problem brought them here?

"Homeowners in Prescott" isn't an audience. It's a census category. Get specific enough that you could pick this person out of a room:

- What just happened that made them search? (Pipe burst. Moving to the area. Business is growing. Old vendor quit.)
- How urgent is it for them: today, this month or "someday"?
- What device are they likely on? (For most local services, a phone, often outdoors or in a truck.)
- What are they afraid of? (Being overcharged. A no-show. Hiring someone unlicensed. Looking foolish for asking.)
- What have they already tried?

**Checklist:**
- [ ] I can describe my best customer in two sentences
- [ ] I can name the trigger that makes them start searching
- [ ] I know their biggest fear about hiring someone like me
- [ ] I know whether they're usually in a hurry or researching

If you serve two very different audiences, for example homeowners *and* commercial property managers, write a profile for each. That's often your first real structural decision: they may each need their own path through the site.

### 3. What does the journey look like, from first search to conversion?

Map the steps a real customer takes. For a typical local service business it might look like this:

1. Searches "[problem] near me" or "[service] [town]" on a phone
2. Sees your Google Business Profile and maybe your website in results
3. Taps through to a page, **not necessarily your homepage**
4. Scans for: do they do my thing, do they serve my area, can I trust them, what does it roughly cost
5. Takes the primary action, or leaves to compare with someone else
6. Maybe returns later via a branded search ("[your business name]")

**Checklist:**
- [ ] I know which searches most likely bring customers to me
- [ ] I know which page they'll most likely land on (often a service page, not the homepage)
- [ ] I've listed the three questions they need answered before acting
- [ ] I know what would make them choose a competitor instead

The landing-page point changes how you plan. Your homepage isn't the front door for everyone. For search visitors, each [service page](/insights/how-to-write-a-service-page-that-ranks) has to do the same job a homepage does: confirm, reassure, convert.

### 4. What proof do you actually have?

Trust signals only work when they're real and specific. Take inventory now so the design can be built around what you have, not around empty placeholder boxes.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Number of Google reviews and average rating
- [ ] Licenses, certifications, insurance, bonding
- [ ] Years in business and owner name/face
- [ ] Real project photos (before/after if relevant)
- [ ] Recognizable local clients or partners (with permission)
- [ ] Guarantees or warranties you actually honor

If this list is thin, that's useful to know *before* building. Fixing it might matter more than the website itself.

### 5. What does the visitor need to know about price, area and availability?

These are the questions people want answered before they'll contact you, and the ones most sites hide:

- [ ] Do I show prices, ranges or "starting at"? (See [should you put prices on your website](/insights/should-you-put-prices-on-your-website).)
- [ ] Is my service area clearly stated, town by town?
- [ ] Are my hours, response times or lead times stated?

## Structuring the Homepage Layout Based on Intent

Now, and only now, you can plan the homepage. Your answers above determine almost every section.

### Above the fold: answer "Am I in the right place?" in three seconds

The top screen on a phone should hold:

- **A plain-language headline that says what you do and where.** "Emergency plumbing in Prescott Valley, 24/7" beats "Excellence in Every Drop." Your answer to Question 2 tells you which words the visitor is looking for.
- **The primary action as a big, obvious button.** From Question 1. If the action is *call*, that's a tap-to-call button, and it should probably stay pinned on screen as the visitor scrolls on mobile.
- **One trust cue.** From Question 4, choose the strongest: a star rating with review count, "Licensed & insured," or years in business.

That's it. No sliders, no autoplay video, no mission statement. A slider is a committee's way of avoiding a decision about what matters most.

### The middle: answer their real questions in the order they ask them

Use your journey map from Question 3. A common order for local service businesses:

1. **What we do**: short service blocks, each linking to its own detailed page
2. **Where we work**: service area, ideally with town names as plain text, not just a map image
3. **Why trust us**: reviews, real photos, credentials, the owner's face
4. **What it costs / what to expect**: price guidance or a simple "how it works" in three steps
5. **The primary action again**, because some people needed the scroll to decide

If you're mostly booking urgent jobs, compress this: trust and the call button matter far more than a detailed process section. If you sell considered, high-ticket work, expand it: process, portfolio and pricing transparency do the persuading.

### The bottom: catch the "not yet" visitors

Your secondary action from Question 1 lives here: a short quote form, a downloadable guide, a link to a project gallery. Put the full name, address and phone number here as plain text too. It helps customers and it helps local search.

### A quick sanity test

When a draft homepage exists, hand your phone to someone who doesn't know your business. Give them five seconds and ask: *What does this company do? Where? What would you tap if you needed them?* If they can't answer all three, the layout isn't done, no matter how good it looks.

## Your One-Page Summary

Before talking to a designer or opening a site builder, you should be able to fill in this sentence:

> "My website helps **[specific customer]** who just **[trigger]** and is worried about **[fear]**. It shows them **[proof]** and gets them to **[primary action]**, mostly from **[device]**, after landing on **[page type]**."
