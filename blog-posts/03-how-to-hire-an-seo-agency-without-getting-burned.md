# How to Hire an SEO Agency Without Getting Burned: 7 Questions to Ask

Ask a room of small business owners about their SEO company and you'll hear one of two stories. Either "I don't really know what they do, but I keep paying," or "I paid for a year and nothing happened."

Both stories share the same problem: the owner couldn't see the work. SEO is unusually easy to fake, because the results are slow, the work is technical and the reports can be made to look busy. This post gives you a framework for vetting an SEO provider *before* you sign, and for checking the work *after*, so you're never in the "I don't really know" camp.

(If an SEO company has promised you first-page or #1 rankings, read [Nobody Can Guarantee You Google Rankings](/insights/nobody-can-guarantee-google-rankings) first. I won't repeat that here.)

## The Red Flags of Shady SEO Providers

Beyond ranking guarantees, here are the patterns that should end a conversation quickly:

- **"Our methods are proprietary."** Legitimate SEO isn't secret. Google publishes its guidelines. A provider who won't tell you what they're doing is usually hiding that it's either very little or very risky.
- **Link packages sold by the number.** "200 backlinks for $199" means automated links from spam sites, private blog networks or comment sections. At best Google ignores them. At worst they earn your site a manual action that takes months to clean up.
- **Unsolicited audits full of "critical errors."** If a stranger emails you a scary report, the errors are usually trivial or false. I've written about [how to read those cold calls and emails](/insights/seo-cold-calls-and-emails-yavapai-county).
- **Long contracts with no exit.** A 12-month lock-in with no performance or deliverable clauses protects the agency, not you.
- **They want admin access to everything, but give you access to nothing.** You should own your Google Search Console, Google Analytics and Google Business Profile, and they should be *users* on your accounts, not the other way round.
- **One price for every business.** A plumber in a town of 20,000 and a law firm in Phoenix don't need the same plan. Identical tiers usually mean identical (templated) work.

## 7 Essential Vetting Questions

### 1. "What specifically will you do in the first 90 days?"

You want a concrete plan with three components:

- **Technical health:** crawl errors, indexing problems, site speed, mobile usability, broken links, redirects, structured data
- **Content:** which pages will be created or improved, targeting which searches, and who writes them
- **Authority:** how they plan to earn links and mentions. For local businesses that means things like local partnerships, sponsorships, industry directories that real people use, and press, not bought links.

**Good answer:** "Month one is an audit and fixing indexing issues. Month two is rewriting your four core service pages and building two new location pages. Month three is citation clean-up and outreach to these local organizations."
**Bad answer:** "We'll optimize your site and build authority."

### 2. "Can you walk me through a result for a business like mine?"

Ask for a case study from a similar business: same type of service, similar market size. Then ask follow-up questions:

- What was the starting point, and what changed?
- How long did it take?
- What did *you* do versus what changed on its own (a new location, an ad campaign, a competitor closing)?
- Can I speak to that client?

Results from a national e-commerce brand tell you nothing about what they'll do for a roofer in Prescott. If they can't show relevant work, ask why they think they're the right fit.

### 3. "How will you report on success, and what metrics matter?"

This is the question that separates real SEO from theatre.

**Vanity metrics** look impressive and mean little: "ranking for 1,400 keywords," "domain authority up 6 points," "impressions up 300%." You can rank for 1,400 keywords nobody searches. Impressions can jump because you started appearing on page six for things no customer types.

**Business metrics** are what you're paying for:

- Organic traffic to **service and location pages** (not just the blog)
- **Calls, form submissions and bookings** from organic search, tracked properly
- Google Business Profile actions: calls, direction requests, website clicks
- Rankings for a *short, agreed list* of searches that actually bring customers

Ask to see a sample report. If it's 30 pages of charts and none of them mention leads, you'll be getting that report every month.

### 4. "What will I be able to see, and in which accounts?"

You should be able to log into your own **Google Search Console** and **Google Analytics** at any time and see the same data they report from. Ask them to set up conversion tracking in *your* accounts during month one. If their answer involves a proprietary dashboard you can only reach through them, be careful: that dashboard disappears the day you leave.

### 5. "What does a typical month of work look like, in hours and deliverables?"

You're not trying to micromanage. You're trying to understand what your retainer buys. A reasonable provider can tell you roughly how the hours split between technical work, content, local listings and outreach, and what you'll receive: a change log, new or updated pages, a list of links earned.

A simple test: ask for a **monthly change log**, a plain list of what was changed on your site and where. Real work leaves a trail.

### 6. "What happens to the optimizations if we part ways?"

Everything done on *your* website and *your* listings should stay yours: content, technical fixes, schema, page improvements, Google Business Profile work. Watch out for:

- Content hosted on *their* domain or a network of their sites
- Links that "go away" when you cancel (a sign they're rented)
- Call-tracking numbers they own, which leave your old number scattered across the web when you leave
- Accounts registered in their name

Get the answer in writing. A confident provider won't mind.

### 7. "What *won't* you do?"

This one's simple but revealing. A good SEO will tell you, without much thought, the tactics they refuse to use: buying links, doorway pages, fake reviews, keyword-stuffed town pages. (On that last one, here's [how to build location pages that don't get penalized](/insights/one-page-per-town-local-landing-pages).) If they hesitate or say "whatever it takes," you have your answer.

## How to Tell If Your *Current* SEO Company Is Actually Doing Work

Already paying someone? You can check without becoming an expert. Spend 15 minutes on this:

1. **Open Google Search Console → Performance.** Compare the last three months to the three before. Are clicks (not just impressions) going up for searches that matter to you? (Here's a [15-minute monthly routine](/insights/google-search-console-guide-for-small-business) if Search Console is new to you.)
2. **Look at your own site.** Can you point to pages that were added or meaningfully improved in the last quarter? If the site looks exactly as it did a year ago, ask what the retainer is paying for.
3. **Check your Google Business Profile.** Is it complete, with recent photos, accurate categories, and replies to reviews?
4. **Ask for the change log and a list of links earned.** Then click a few of those links. Real links sit on real local or industry sites that a human would read.
5. **Ask what's planned for next month and why.** A provider doing real work always has a next step tied to a reason.

If two or more of these come up empty, it's time for a direct conversation, or a second opinion.

## Setting Realistic Timelines: Why Legitimate SEO Takes 4 to 6 Months

The most honest thing an SEO provider can tell you is that **you probably won't see meaningful results in the first 90 days**, and here's why:

- **Months 1–2: fixing and building.** Audits, technical fixes, rewriting pages, setting up tracking. Google needs time to recrawl and re-evaluate changed pages, and on smaller sites that can take weeks.
- **Months 3–4: early movement.** Rankings start shifting for less competitive, specific searches: long-tail and location-plus-service terms. Impressions often climb before clicks do.
- **Months 4–6: compounding.** Better pages earn better engagement, which earns links and mentions, which lifts related pages. This is when enquiries from organic search usually become noticeable for a local business.
- **Month 6 and beyond:** steadier growth, and room for more competitive terms.

These are typical ranges, not promises. A brand-new domain in a crowded market takes longer. An established business whose site has obvious technical problems can see faster gains once they're fixed. What you should expect from month one is *visible activity and clear reporting*, even before the results arrive.

The flip side: if you're nine months in and the provider can't show progress in business metrics, and can't explain why, patience has stopped being the right strategy.

## The Short Version

Hire the SEO provider who will show you the work, report on leads rather than vanity numbers, leave everything in your name and give you an honest timeline. Walk away from anyone selling secrets, guarantees or link bundles.
