# Traditional SEO vs. AI Search: How to Adapt Your Website Strategy

"Is SEO dead?" comes up every few months, and it's especially loud now that AI-written summaries sit at the top of many Google results and plenty of people ask ChatGPT, Gemini or Perplexity instead of searching at all.

Short answer: no. AI tools still get their information from websites, and search still sends people to businesses. What *has* changed is which websites get chosen. The tactics that worked in 2018, such as repeating a keyword, writing thin pages for every variation and padding posts to a word count, matter less every year. What wins now is depth, clarity and a site people actually find useful.

I've covered [what Google's AI Overviews change for local business websites](/insights/ai-overviews-and-local-business-websites) separately: crawler access, Bing, and why you shouldn't buy "AI SEO" packages. This post is about the bigger strategic shift and what to do with your content and structure because of it.

## The Shift in Search: What's Different From Five Years Ago

Five years ago, simplifying a little, ranking a page meant matching it to a keyword. The search engine looked for pages that used the words, had some links pointing at them and loaded reasonably well. A clever page could rank for a term even if the rest of the site had nothing to say about the subject.

Today three things work differently:

**1. Search engines understand topics, not just words.** Modern systems work out what a page is *about* and how it relates to other concepts. A page about "water heater replacement" is understood alongside tankless vs. tank, sizing, hard water, permits and cost. Exact-match phrasing matters much less than whether you've actually covered the subject.

**2. Answers are assembled, not just listed.** AI summaries pull specific passages from several sources and combine them into one answer. Your page may get cited for a single paragraph, or skipped entirely if the useful fact is buried under 400 words of introduction.

**3. The bar for "useful" has gone up.** When an AI can write a generic article in seconds, generic articles are worth almost nothing. What stands out is what an AI can't invent: first-hand experience, local specifics, real numbers, real photos and clear positions. (This is the thinking behind [E-E-A-T](/insights/e-e-a-t-for-local-businesses).)

## What Matters Now

### Topical Authority vs. Keyword Density

**Keyword density**, the old idea that a page should repeat its target phrase a certain percentage of the time, is effectively dead. Stuffing now reads as low quality to people and machines alike.

**Topical authority** has replaced it: the sense, built across your whole site, that you're a thorough and reliable source on a subject. You build it with **content clusters**:

- **A pillar page** that covers a core service in depth. For example: "Roof Replacement in Prescott Valley."
- **Cluster pages** that each answer one specific question in that topic: "How long does a roof last in Northern Arizona's climate?", "Metal vs. asphalt shingles for high-desert homes," "What does a roof inspection include?", "Do I need a permit to replace my roof in Yavapai County?"
- **Internal links** that connect each cluster page to the pillar and to each other where it makes sense. (More on that in [internal linking, explained simply](/insights/internal-linking-for-small-business-websites).)

For a small business, this doesn't mean publishing hundreds of articles. It means choosing **two or three services that make you the most money** and covering each one properly, rather than writing thin posts on 40 loosely related topics.

A practical way to find cluster topics: write down every question customers ask you on the phone, on site and in emails for a month. Those questions are your content plan, and they're phrased exactly the way people type them into search and AI tools.

### Generative Engine Optimization (GEO): Writing So AI Can Use It

Generative Engine Optimization is the practice of structuring content so AI-driven search can easily understand, quote and cite it. There's no secret markup involved. Mostly it's good writing with discipline:

- **Answer first, then explain.** Put the direct answer in the first sentence or two under each heading. "A typical asphalt roof in the Prescott area lasts about 20 to 25 years; here's what shortens it." AI systems pull passages, and a passage that opens with the answer is easy to lift.
- **One idea per section, with a descriptive heading.** Headings phrased as the question ("How much does a tankless water heater cost to install?") act as labels that both people and machines can scan.
- **Make claims specific and checkable.** "We've served Dewey-Humboldt since 2009" is citable. "We're the best in the business" is not. Specific numbers, ranges, time frames and conditions get used. Vague superlatives get ignored.
- **Use tables and lists for comparisons.** A table comparing options on cost, lifespan and maintenance is ideal for AI to pull from, and it's genuinely more useful to readers.
- **Show who's talking.** Author names, credentials, real project photos and an About page that says who you are all help systems judge whether to trust you. (See [schema markup explained](/insights/schema-markup-local-business-explained) for the technical side of labeling this information.)
- **Publish what only you know.** Local conditions, your own pricing ranges, common mistakes you see on job sites, how permitting works in your county. Original information is the hardest thing for AI answers to get anywhere else, which makes it the most likely to be cited.

One honest caveat: measuring GEO is still crude. You can't reliably tell how often an AI tool mentions your business. Treat these as quality practices that also help traditional search, not as a separate channel to chase.

### User Experience (UX) as an SEO Factor

Search engines want to send people to pages that satisfy them. Google has said publicly that page experience is part of its systems, and its ranking systems are designed to favor content people find helpful. You don't need to know the exact signals to see the pattern: **a site that frustrates people loses ground over time.**

The usual culprits:

- **Slow loading, especially on mobile.** If the page takes too long, people go back and choose another result. ([Core Web Vitals](/insights/core-web-vitals-explained-for-small-business) are Google's own measure of this.)
- **Layouts built for a desktop.** Tiny tap targets, text you have to zoom to read and pop-ups that cover the content on a phone.
- **Content that doesn't match the search.** If someone searches "emergency electrician" and lands on a page about your company history, they'll leave quickly, and the page doesn't deserve to rank for that search. ([Search intent](/insights/search-intent-explained-for-local-businesses) matters more than the keyword.)
- **Dead ends.** Pages with no clear next step: no related links, no call to action, no way forward.

A note on bounce rate: a high bounce rate isn't automatically bad. Someone who finds your phone number and calls immediately counts as a "bounce." The real problem is people leaving *unsatisfied*. Look at bounce rate alongside time on page and conversions before you panic about it.

The good news for small businesses: UX is an area where you can beat bigger competitors. A fast, clear, mobile-first site from a local company often gives a better experience than a national directory full of ads.

## Actionable Takeaways: Audit Your Site for Modern Search

Set aside an afternoon and work through this:

**Content and authority**
- [ ] List your top three money-making services. Does each have its own in-depth page?
- [ ] For each, list five to ten real customer questions. How many does your site answer?
- [ ] Do related pages link to each other and back to the main service page?
- [ ] Look for thin pages (a few sentences, duplicated town pages) that should be merged, expanded or removed.

**GEO readiness**
- [ ] Do your key pages answer the main question in the first two sentences?
- [ ] Are headings descriptive, and phrased as questions where it fits?
- [ ] Do you include specifics only you can provide: local details, price ranges, time frames, real photos?
- [ ] Is it clear who wrote or stands behind the content?
- [ ] Are your business name, address, phone and service area identical everywhere they appear?

**User experience**
- [ ] Run your homepage and top service page through [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/) on mobile.
- [ ] Open each key page on your phone: is the main action visible without scrolling?
- [ ] In Google Search Console, find pages with plenty of impressions but few clicks. Rewrite their [titles and descriptions](/insights/title-tags-and-meta-descriptions-that-get-clicks) and check that the content matches what people searched for.
- [ ] Remove or delay pop-ups on mobile.

**Measurement**
- [ ] Confirm that calls and form submissions are tracked as conversions.
- [ ] Check the referral traffic report in analytics for visits from AI tools (chatgpt.com, perplexity.ai and similar). The numbers are small for most local businesses today, but worth watching.

## The Bottom Line

Traditional SEO and AI search aren't opposites. Both reward the same thing: a website that covers its subject thoroughly, says useful things clearly and is pleasant to use. The businesses that lose ground are those still relying on shortcuts. The ones that gain are those that write down what they actually know and make it easy to find.
